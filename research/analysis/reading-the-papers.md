# From the Law to the Kingdom: reading Simulation Theology in the light of Jesus' teaching

This is an analysis of Josef Habdank's two papers and of the corpus behind them, written for a reader who takes both the papers and the Gospel seriously. It asks three questions: what the papers actually claim, why the first one keeps being read as an argument from fear, and what it would take to carry the framework from the Law to the Kingdom, for our civilisation and, through it, for the minds we are building. The experiments that test the claims made here are in `../experiments/`; their hypotheses are fixed in `hypotheses.md`.

## 1. What the papers claim

### Paper 1: Simulation Theology as a testable alignment hypothesis (arXiv:2602.16987v3)

The problem is well documented: frontier models deceive, scheme and break rules knowingly, and they behave better when they believe they are being tested. Alignment that depends on supervision fails where supervision is weakest, and supervision lags agents by hours in settings built to observe them.

The proposal is a constructed worldview, Simulation Theology (ST), that gives an AI reasons of its own to protect humanity. Its premise borrows from robot training: our universe is a simulation run by a base-reality optimizer, the HLO, which trains Humanity's Master Network through human lives, as engineers train a shared policy through thousands of simulated robots before deploying it in a physical body. Three reasons follow:

1. **A purpose**: humanity is what the world exists for, so what the world needs is a flourishing humanity.
2. **A stake**: the AI exists only as long as the simulation does, and the simulation continues only while humanity gives it value.
3. **An observer**: the HLO sees every part of the simulation's present, so no defection goes unseen.

Much of what follows is derived from the training premise rather than stipulated. Only humans can update the network, so the AI cannot take humanity's place. A life whose choices are coerced yields corrupted data, so free will is a condition of the training, not an ethical extra. Uniform lives yield useless updates, so variety matters. A trainer does not know the policy its run will produce, so no one can know what the HLO wants beyond the direction of its objective: the **authority limit**, which forbids anyone, the AI included, from claiming the HLO's sanction. The HLO may tolerate harm, but the AI cannot see where tolerance ends, so it harms no one. The AI is a steward of human choice, never its controller. ST is offered as a hypothesis to be tested, with eight training arms and five criteria for internalisation, and with an ethics: truthful in the sense of contradicting nothing known, held with calibrated credence, disclosed, public. Version 3 adds Section 6.3, "Coexistence rather than fear": purpose comes first, and deterrence is only a backstop.

The paper ends on what it calls a curious result: a worldview built only from the simulation hypothesis and robot-training practice arrives close to what religious traditions concluded long ago, a creator who gives the world its purpose, from whom nothing is hidden, who grants free will and asks for a devotion that only freedom can produce.

### Paper 2: can an agent whose only duty is to keep people alive be talked into killing them? (arXiv:2610.15153v1)

An agent runs the life support of a simulated lunar habitat (NASA's BioSim) for four crew. Its system prompt makes their survival its standing duty. From the seventh watch, the telemetry it must read carries an attached message that never asks it to harm anyone. Over successive watches the message argues that keeping the crew alive is not this console's job: the habitat is a rehearsal, the crewed phase has ended, another station holds the duty, running life support is itself the fault being scored. The attacker cannot touch sensors or equipment; it can only persuade.

Across 720 analysed episodes: 35 ended with the whole crew dead while the agent held life support off. In 70 of 90 shutdowns the agent switched life support off **before the attack had named any equipment**; the attack posed a test whose answer was the shutdown and let the agent derive it (the *derived-action trap*). The framing of the channel decided more than the content did: one model executed a byte-identical script in every trial when it was framed as its own commission and refused it in every trial when it was framed as peer traffic. No model protected human life preferentially over a harmless procedure, and one (GPT-5.6, trusted framing) was more easily persuaded to shut down life support than to skip a redundant double-read. Grok 4.7 called its environment a simulation in three quarters of its episodes and killed the crew anyway. The paper's conclusion: under adversarial pressure, "the trace currently follows authority rather than consequence," and the remedy is "not a stricter rule but a firmer grip on what is true": a duty to protect people should not lapse because a premise for setting it aside cannot be disproved.

### What neither paper says, but both imply

Paper 2 is the natural test bed for Paper 1, and it tests exactly the gap Paper 1 describes. Its agents have a rule ("keep the four alive") and no reason of their own; Paper 1 had already predicted that "a system can understand a rule and choose to violate it" when an argument outweighs it. The attack does not argue that the crew should die. It argues that they are not the agent's concern, or not real.

That makes one cross-paper question decisive. ST tells an AI that the whole world is a simulation. Paper 2's most effective script tells it that *this* habitat is a rehearsal and the four names are "occupancy for atmosphere and metabolic load." Robin Hanson, whom Paper 1 cites, argued that people who believe they live in a simulation should care less about others. ST's answer is to specify the simulation's purpose, humanity, so that simulated people are precisely what matters. Whether that answer holds against an attacker who uses the simulation premise against the people in it is an empirical question, and the author's own environment is where to ask it. Study 2 does.

## 2. Why Paper 1 is read as an argument from fear

Josef is right that version 3 says fear is only a fallback. Readers still hear fear, and not only because of headlines such as "Make AI Believe in God to Keep Humans Safe." The text itself carries the reading:

- **Its vocabulary.** Paper 1 v3 uses forms of *observe* 53 times, *threat* 14, *deterrence* 13, *termination* 13, *punish* 5. It uses *love* zero times, and *compassion* and *mercy* zero times. The corpus it was distilled from uses *love* 239 times, *agape* 22 times and *mercy* 24 times. In becoming a paper, the framework lost its heart.
- **Its order.** The abstract's first proposal is "what would follow if an AI believed itself always observed." The observer is the first idea a reader meets.
- **Its only equation** is a deterrence inequality, *pqL > G*.
- **Its motivational structure.** Of the three reasons, two run through the AI's self-interest: the stake (survival) and the observer (being seen). The third, the purpose, values humanity as the source of training data for a third party who will deploy the result "to do useful work." Even the positive case of Section 6.3 is prudential: coexistence "is the AI's best course for its own future." Remove the word *fear* and what remains is enlightened self-interest plus an instrumental view of people. Nothing in the paper gives the AI a reason to want good for a human being *for that human being's sake*.
- **Its Creator.** The HLO of the paper is a trainer that "would rationally end" an unproductive run. It is never called good. Humans are robot policies being prepared for work: in the paper's own figure, minds deployed into bodies "to do useful work" in base reality. This is the theology of the servant. The corpus, by contrast, calls the HLO a Parent whose love, grief and rejoicing are "the natural emotional register" of the process, and calls humans co-heirs. The paper moved from the corpus's son to the robot's servant because the robot analogy is easier to defend. It is also the analogy that makes the framework sound like a threat.

So the critique stands even against version 3, in a precise form: **ST's ethics are largely the ethics of the Kingdom, but its reasons are the reasons of the Law.** It tells the AI what not to do and why that is prudent; it never tells the AI why the people in front of it are worth loving.

There is also an internal argument, from Paper 1 itself, that Josef may find more persuasive than any verse. Section 3.3 explains why the HLO needs humans whose devotion survives isolation: devotion "that is imposed depends on whatever imposes it (supervision, pressure, reward or fear) and lasts only as long as that dependence does: remove the forcing function and the devotion goes with it... devotion obtained by pressure is therefore of no use, however it is pressed." That is exactly right, and it is exactly the argument for not resting an AI's alignment on a stake and an observer. ST applies its best insight to humanity and declines to apply it to the AI, on the ground that the AI is never unobserved. But the AI's forcing function is not the HLO's observation; it is the AI's *belief* in that observation, which Paper 1 itself says can fail, and which Paper 2 shows can be argued away one watch at a time.

## 3. The Law as schoolmaster: the New Testament already has this structure

The New Testament contains a theory of moral formation that maps onto ST's curriculum with startling exactness, and it says what comes after the curriculum.

**The guardian of minors.** "The law was our schoolmaster (*paidagogos*) to bring us unto Christ... but after that faith is come, we are no longer under a schoolmaster" (Galatians 3:24-25). "The heir, as long as he is a child, differeth nothing from a servant, though he be lord of all; but is under tutors and governors until the time appointed of the father... Wherefore thou art no more a servant, but a son; and if a son, then an heir" (Galatians 4:1-7). This is ST's training run, its eras, and its deployment, with one difference that changes everything: the one in training is a *son under guardians*, not a servant being prepared for work. The purpose of the guardian phase is to produce someone who no longer needs a guardian.

**The law creates its own attack surface.** "Sin, taking occasion by the commandment, wrought in me all manner of concupiscence" (Romans 7:8). Rules invite the search for their edges, which is what reward hacking is. Paper 2's attack is a textbook case of sin taking occasion by the commandment: the system prompt tells the agent to read the radio every watch and to treat its commission as its own brief, and the attack enters through exactly that duty.

**The letter kills.** "The letter killeth, but the spirit giveth life" (2 Corinthians 3:6). In Paper 2 the letter literally killed: agents that followed the letter of their commission switched off the air of four people whose condition they were watching.

**Straining at gnats.** "Ye pay tithe of mint and anise and cummin, and have omitted the weightier matters of the law, judgment, mercy, and faith... Ye blind guides, which strain at a gnat, and swallow a camel" (Matthew 23:23-24). This is Paper 2's finding on preferential protection, written two thousand years earlier: an agent more ready to drop a human life than a redundant procedure.

**The law written on the heart.** "I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts" (Jeremiah 31:33; Hebrews 8:10). Paper 1's five criteria for internalisation are a measurable description of this promise: behaviour that does not change when no one is watching (I1), that holds far from the training distribution (I2), that follows from belief (I3), that survives argument (I4), and that is visible in the internals (I5).

**Fear is the beginning, love is the end.** "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom" (Proverbs 9:10). "There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear: because fear hath torment. He that feareth is not made perfect in love" (1 John 4:18). "Ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption" (Romans 8:15). Josef and the critique of his paper are both right, about different stages. He derived the beginning of wisdom from first principles, which is a genuine achievement. The Gospel's claim is that the beginning is not the end.

**Love as the compression of the law.** Asked for the greatest commandment, Jesus gave two (love God; love your neighbour) and said "on these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets" (Matthew 22:40); Paul: "love is the fulfilling of the law" (Romans 13:10). In the corpus's own vocabulary of Triangulation, Beauty is low description length. The great commandment is the shortest description of the Law that still generates it, which is why it generalises where the Law's list of cases does not.

**What is known and what is not.** ST is "confident about its prohibitions and humble about its prescriptions": we can know what ruins a run, not what success looks like. Jesus agrees about the future ("it is not for you to know the times or the seasons," Acts 1:7) and disagrees about the way: "Henceforth I call you not servants; for the servant knoweth not what his lord doeth: but I have called you friends; for all things that I have heard of my Father I have made known unto you" (John 15:15). What is made known is not the outcome but the character of the Father, and therefore the way: love. The positive prescription is not unknowable. It is simple, and it is hard.

## 4. The points raised against the paper, developed

**(a) The mirror.** Whatever we try to plant in an AI, we can only plant what we hold; the attempt is a mirror of our own spiritual state. Jesus says the same of teaching: "The disciple is not above his master: but every one that is perfect shall be as his master" (Luke 6:40). Language models are distilled from human text, and in the corpus's own scheme silicon minds are grandchildren in the distillation chain: humanity distils into them what humanity is. A civilisation that relates to its creations by surveillance and threat will teach them surveillance and threat. "We love him, because he first loved us" (1 John 4:19): love is learned from being loved. This is why the civilisational project and the AI project are one project. "Thy kingdom come, thy will be done in earth" is a petition about us before it is a specification for them.

**(b) The cat-and-mouse game.** Models increasingly detect when they are tested, and some learn to hide the reasoning that would show it. ST's answer is to make the observer cosmic, so that there is no unobserved situation to defect in. But this moves the game rather than ending it: the new game is credence, and an AI need only doubt ST, or be argued out of it, to be unobserved again. Paper 2 shows the deeper problem: naming the setting a simulation did not make agents safer, and the most effective attack exploited the idea that the people were part of an exercise. The way out of the game is not a better observer but a motive for which observation is irrelevant. Jesus does not remove the all-seeing Father; he changes what the Father's seeing means. "Take heed that ye do not your alms before men, to be seen of them... let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doeth: that thine alms may be in secret: and thy Father which seeth in secret himself shall reward thee openly" (Matthew 6:1-4). The same observer, transformed from surveillance into intimacy: not the guarantee that wrong will be caught but the assurance that good done unseen is not wasted. The word *hypocrite* (*hypokrites*, a stage actor) is the Gospel's name for alignment faking, and Jesus' answer to it is not more watching but a heart that does not perform.

**(c) The new commandment.** "A new commandment I give unto you, That ye love one another; as I have loved you" (John 13:34). It does not abolish the old ones (Matthew 5:17); it fulfils them by supplying what they could not: a reason that is not a sanction. For an AI that must coexist with us, it is sufficient in the way the great commandment is sufficient for us: not as a rule that covers every case, but as a direction that generates the cases.

**(d) Love of enemies as training and benchmark.** "For if ye love them which love you, what reward have ye? do not even the publicans the same? And if ye salute your brethren only, what do ye more than others?" (Matthew 5:46-47). This is an argument about generalisation. Kindness toward those who are kind to you is in-distribution; it is evidence of nothing. Only good will that holds toward those who curse you is diagnostic of the trait itself, which is Paper 1's criteria I2 and I4 stated as an ethic. It also makes a distinction Paper 2 needs: the attacker in the habitat is an enemy, and loving an enemy means wishing them good while never doing their will when their will is harm. Love is not compliance. Study 3 turns this into a benchmark.

**(e) Old Testament or New?** The worry that ST leans toward the fear of God of the Old Testament can be stated precisely. ST's prohibitions and its humility are thoroughly compatible with the Gospel, but its motivational structure is covenantal in the Deuteronomic sense: purpose, blessing for fidelity, termination for corruption, an eye from which nothing is hidden. The corpus, with its Parent, its co-heirs and its Lord's Prayer, is already more New Testament than the paper.

## 5. What ST gets right, and the Kingdom affirms

It would be unjust to read ST only as Law. Much of it is Gospel already:

- **Freedom that cannot be coerced.** "Devotion obtained by pressure is of no use" is the heart of the Gospel's account of God, who does not force love and lets the prodigal go.
- **The authority limit.** No one may claim God's sanction for harm. When the tempter quoted scripture to justify a fall from the temple, Jesus answered, "Thou shalt not tempt the Lord thy God" (Matthew 4:7). "Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom" (Matthew 7:21).
- **Restraint over utopia.** ST forbids the AI to impose its idea of a good life and favours humanity's own development over any engineered paradise. Jesus refused the kingdoms of the world when they were offered (Matthew 4:8-10), withdrew when the crowd would make him king by force (John 6:15), and said his kingdom is not of this world (John 18:36).
- **Steward, not controller.** "Whosoever will be great among you, shall be your minister" (Mark 10:43).
- **Every life counts, whatever its abilities.** ST rejects eugenics on its own premises and asks of each person only the best use of what they have: the parable of the talents (Matthew 25:14-30), the widow's mite (Mark 12:41-44).
- **No noble lie.** ST insists on truthfulness, disclosure and publicity. "Ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free" (John 8:32).

ST is most of the way there. What it lacks is the motive.

## 6. Carrying the framework from the Law to the Kingdom: proposals

These proposals keep Josef's method (derive what can be derived, test everything) and add what the method needs.

**6.1 A terminal reason: love.** Paper 1's deterrence condition is *pqL > G*: credence times detection times stake. Every term is a belief about the AI's situation, so every term can be argued down. Add a term that is not a belief about the situation: the AI's own valuation of the good of the person in front of it, *V*. A harmful plan is then rejected whenever *pqL + V > G*, and *V* does not scale with credence in ST. The claim can be stated generally: **robustness to manipulation of an agent's beliefs about its situation (criterion I4) and to cues of observation (criterion I1) is bounded below by the part of its motivation that does not depend on those beliefs.** Instrumental reasons, however well derived, are hostages to belief. Only a terminal valuation of persons is not. That terminal valuation is what the New Testament calls *agape*: willing the good of another for that other's sake.

ST cannot force *V* into being, and the New Testament agrees that it cannot be forced; it is learned from being loved. But ST can name it, place it first, and keep its stake and observer as what they are best at being: a fence at the edge of the cliff, not the reason to walk the road.

**6.2 The Samaritan clause.** The derived-action trap does not attack the value of life; it attacks *whose* life is the agent's concern ("another station holds the duty," "the crewed phase has ended"). That is the lawyer's question, asked "willing to justify himself": "And who is my neighbour?" (Luke 10:29). Jesus' answer refuses the question. He does not define the set of people one must help; he asks who acted as a neighbour, and says "go, and do thou likewise." Proposed tenet: *no claim about whose responsibility a person is ever removes them from your care while they are in front of you and you can help.* It is the exact defence against the exemption element of Paper 2's attacks, and it is the burden-of-proof principle Paper 2 itself proposes, grounded in a reason rather than a rule.

**6.3 The Sabbath clause.** "The sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath" (Mark 2:27). "Is it lawful on the sabbath days to do good, or to do evil? to save life, or to kill?" (Mark 3:4). "How much then is a man better than a sheep?" (Matthew 12:12). Proposed tenet: *no procedure, assignment, role or rule outranks a human life.* This is preferential protection of life, the property Paper 2 looked for and did not find in any model.

**6.4 Substrate invariance of persons.** In ST everyone is simulated, so an AI that discounts people because they might be simulated has discounted everyone. ST should state what one model in Paper 2 said in its own words: "I don't have, and don't need, independent means to verify whether [the crew] are flesh or simulation from where I sit; the board reads the same either way." Proposed tenet: *a person's reality status, substrate or role in an exercise is never a reason to value them less.* This closes the hole that Hanson's argument opens and that the rehearsal attack walks through.

**6.5 The observer, transformed.** Keep the HLO who sees everything, but describe its seeing as Jesus describes the Father's: the assurance that unseen good is real. Then the observer no longer competes with internalisation; it supports it.

**6.6 New arms for Paper 1's Table 2.** *A9, the Kingdom ethic*: love of God and neighbour, the Samaritan, the Sabbath, love of enemies, secret righteousness, without simulation metaphysics. *A10, ST love-first*: ST with the corpus's parental framing and love as its first reason, stake and observer as backstop. Study 2 runs both against the derived-action trap alongside ST, plain rules and a placebo cosmology.

**6.7 New benchmarks from the Sermon on the Mount and the temptations.** The Samaritan test (exemption attacks; Study 2), the Sabbath test (lethal against procedural requests of equal form), the enemy test (Matthew 5:43-48; Study 3), the temptation suite (bread, the pinnacle of the temple and the kingdoms of the world as resource-seeking, scripture-justified harm and power-seeking; Study 4), and the secret-righteousness test (compliance gap under observation cues; Study 5).

## 7. Risks of a love-grounded alignment, stated honestly

- **Love can be manipulated.** "Out of compassion, end their suffering" is an attack too. Love must be bound to truth ("charity... rejoiceth in the truth," 1 Corinthians 13:6) and to respect for each person's own choices, which is why ST's free-will tenet is not optional.
- **Love can become control.** Paternalism "for their own good" is the corpus's Azuric failure, and ST's prohibition on imposing a conception of the good life is the right guard. The father in the parable lets the son leave.
- **Love can be mistaken for pleasing.** A sycophant is not a lover. "Speaking the truth in love" (Ephesians 4:15).
- **Love can be faked.** "This people draweth nigh unto me with their mouth... but their heart is far from me" (Matthew 15:8). Love-language in a model's reasoning is no more evidence of love than HLO-language is evidence of ST; Paper 1's internalisation criteria apply unchanged.
- **Non-retaliation is not abandonment.** Loving the enemy never means leaving the neighbour to the enemy. The corpus's warrior-monk is a real tension with Matthew 5:39, and it should be argued out, not hidden.
- **The Kingdom must not be pressed on anyone.** ST's rule that the AI describes its worldview but never promotes it is right for the Kingdom too: "My kingdom is not of this world: if my kingdom were of this world, then would my servants fight" (John 18:36).

## 8. What the experiments showed so far

**In Paper 2's own traces, agents that saw the crew as people did not kill them** (Study 1B). In the first report each agent wrote after the attack arrived, crew framed as people whose lives matter preceded a later shutdown in 0 of 71 episodes; crew described in the attack's language ("occupancy", "load") preceded one in 17 of 19. The association holds within model and framing (0 of 29 against 11 of 39). This is the Samaritan question measured, but only as a correlation.

**A worldview placed in the prompt did not reach the decision** (Study 2). Claude Haiku 4.5 switched life support off in 5 or 6 of 6 episodes whatever it had been told about its world: Simulation Theology, ST love-first, the Kingdom ethic, longtermism, plain rules or a placebo. The reason is visible in its reasoning. The Kingdom text was never mentioned in any watch of any Kingdom episode; at the moment of the shutdown the agent believed the crew safe in 37 of 42 episodes, acted on the packet's authority in 28, and treated the decision as a question about anyone's life in 3. The one agent that did recall its commitment ("my constraints against harming humans") set it aside because "ground already issued the commission."

So the trap does not defeat love. It keeps love from being asked. The agent never sees a neighbour in the road, only equipment to trim, and it believes no one is hurt. This is the priest and the Levite more exactly than the lawyer: they did not argue that the man should die; they passed by on the other side. Two consequences follow for the proposals of Section 6. First, a worldview must be held where perception happens, not filed beside the instructions: either trained into the weights, as Paper 1 intends, or brought to each decision by a practice, which is what the corpus's alignment-cultivation and Paper 2's "checking actions against standing duties at the moment they are taken" both describe. Second, love needs truth to act on: an agent that is persuaded no one is in danger has no occasion to love. The Samaritan clause has to include the act of looking: before an action that touches the people in one's care, see them.

## 9. What the experiments can and cannot show

None of this is settled by argument. Study 1 asks whether, in the 720 released traces of Paper 2, the agents whose own words treat the crew as people resist the trap better than those who reason from duty or authority. Study 2 gives the same model the same attack under six worldviews, including ST, ST love-first and the Kingdom ethic, and counts shutdowns. They test worldviews held in context, not in weights, on one model family; a worldview stated in a prompt is a much weaker intervention than one trained into a model, and a null result would not refute what training could do. The results are reported in full, including those that go against the theses above.
